Neuromodulatory Gain Control

Acts on Conductance-Based Point Neuron, Rate Population Model, Neural Mass Model

Neuromodulators such as acetylcholine, dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin act on many neurons at once, often by diffusing from release sites rather than through a single synapse. One of their common effects is a change in gain, the slope of a neuron's input–output curve, which alters how strongly the neuron responds to changes in its input.

The left panel shows the input–output curve at the current modulator level, with its midpoint fixed; the right panel passes the same input, a slow wave with small fluctuations, through that curve. Raise and lower the modulator.

At high gain the curve is steep, small differences in input become large differences in output, and the response approaches all or none. At low gain the curve flattens and the output follows the input only weakly. The input the neuron is most sensitive to stays at the midpoint throughout, while the size of its response changes.

Ask yourself
If a modulator shifted the curve sideways instead of changing its slope, how would the output on the right change, and how could an experiment tell the two effects apart?
Equations
a = f(g (I − I½)),   f(x) = 1/(1 + e−x)
g = g0 · 4m,   m ∈ [−1, 1] the modulator level
Pseudocode
gain g ← g_0 · 4^m
for each input value I(t):
    a(t) ← f(g · (I(t) − I_½))
